Turn 4 · 3b · where the af sits

Four ways to put it in the corner

Same 4:5 frame, same italic af, four different relationships to the bottom-right. The corner is the right instinct — it reads as a signature or a date stamp on a print rather than a letter centred in a box. What separates these is whether the letters sit inside the frame, on it, or outside it.

4a · CHOSENTucked inside

Letters sit fully within the frame, optically inset from both edges — a photographer's corner signature. The frame stays a closed object, which is what makes it read as a mat holding something. At 18px the letters become a single solid square in the corner, which is legible as a mark rather than as type.

Committed. The af is inset 19% of the frame width from the right rule and 8% of the height from the bottom, so the f's terminal and descender both clear the frame with air to spare — no letterform touches a rule at any size.

4bBreaking the rule

The af straddles the bottom rule and knocks a gap in it, the way a caption plate interrupts a border or a date is burned into the edge of a negative. This is the one with actual tension — the frame is no longer a plain rectangle, and the letters are clearly on the object rather than in it. Strongest at large sizes and on the sign-in page.

Trade: the knockout needs the background colour behind the letters, so it can't be a single-colour glyph — it needs a filled variant for embroidery or a stamp. At 18px it degrades to a notch.

4cSigned beneath

Frame left completely clean; the af sits under its bottom-right corner, right-aligned to the edge — a signature in the margin of a mounted print. The frame becomes a true empty mat, which is the most honest version of the idea: the product is the container, the photograph is the content.

Trade: two separate shapes, so the lockup is taller and needs more clear space. In a 190px nav rail it either shrinks or loses the letters.

4dCorner tab

The letters sit in a filled block seated into the bottom-right corner — a tab or index label on a file. Highest contrast of the four and the only one whose corner is a solid, so it holds shape at any size and in any single colour. Degrades to a frame with a filled corner at 18px, which is still unmistakably the same mark.

Trade: the heaviest. The solid corner competes with the frame for attention, and it's one step closer to looking like a UI badge than a wordmark.

My read

4a is the mark, with the af pulled left so no letterform touches a rule. It is a single-colour glyph with no knockout, which means one asset works in ink, reversed, ochre, embroidered and at favicon size. Construction and usage are specified in §10 of ClearAF-Letterpress-Spec.md; the other three stay here as the record of what was considered.

Now applied to the mobile sign-in, the portal nav rail, the portal sign-in and this guide's masthead. The iOS app icon must be redrawn full-bleed at 1024 with no baked corner radius, plus dark and tinted variants — see the spec.

Turn 3 · identity · five marks

A mark that belongs to Letterpress

All five are typographic or geometric — no illustration, no abstract swoosh, nothing that needs explaining. Each is shown as a wordmark, then as an app icon at 76 / 36 / 18px, in ink and reversed, so you can judge it at the size it will actually be seen. Reply with an id.

LUCIDE ICON

The app icon is a single 1024px raster — an AI-generated line-art profile with a cyan→blue arc on near-black, and AppIcon.appiconset/Contents.json holds just that one entry. Four problems: the corner radius and dark plate are baked into the pixels, so iOS masks an already-rounded image; the gradient arc is the retired purple/teal lineage (generate_icon.py still generates #6B46C1 → #06B6D4); there are no dark or tinted variants; and at 18px the face collapses while the arc survives, so the mark reads as a blue crescent. Separately, the portal has no logo at all — public/ contains only Next.js defaults, and the sidebar uses lucide's Stethoscope glyph. So there are two unrelated marks, and one of them is a stock icon.

A literal face also competes with the product: every screen is already full of the patient's actual face. The mark's job is to frame that, not to draw it.

3bThe 4:5 frame

A bare 4:5 rectangle — the exact aspect of every photograph in the product — with the italic af sitting on its baseline. It means something specific: this is the thing that holds your face over time. At 18px the letters drop out and the frame alone carries it, which is how a real mark should degrade.

Trade: a hairline rectangle is quiet; it needs the 2px weight at large sizes to not look like a placeholder box.

3dFull stop

A solid ink square sits between the two words as punctuation — a period with a square cut, the same shape as the calendar's "recorded" cell. The icon is that square inverted, notched once so it isn't just a rectangle. Simplest possible geometry: prints, embroiders, engraves, survives a 16px favicon intact.

Trade: a square is a square. It only becomes yours through repetition, and you'd want the notch used consistently or dropped.

3eTwo of four

A 2×2 grid with two cells filled — a contact sheet, and the same three-state logic as the adherence strip and calendar. It's the one mark here that encodes the product's actual idea (a record built from repeated days) in pure geometry, and it holds perfectly at 18px. Shown with an optional mono descriptor for the App Store and the portal sign-in.

Trade: 2×2 grids are common in software marks. The diagonal fill is what keeps it from reading as a generic "dashboard" glyph — don't let it become four equal squares.

My read

3b is the one I'd pick: the 4:5 frame is the product, it's unmistakably yours the moment you see it next to a photo grid, and it degrades honestly. 3e is the strongest conceptually and the best small — pick it if you want the mark to carry meaning without the wordmark. 3d is the most practical for stamps, embroidery and favicons. 3a is the no-risk option if you'd rather the app have no icon-mark at all. 3c only if you want to lean fully clinical and give up the serif.
